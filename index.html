What Is Trauma?

Explore the definition below alongside your initial understanding.

Your Understanding

SAMHSA's Definition

Individual trauma results from an event, series of events, or set of circumstances that is experienced by an individual as physically or emotionally harmful or life threatening and that has lasting adverse effects on the individual’s functioning and mental, physical, social, emotional, or spiritual well-being.

Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA, 2014, p. 7).

Event

What happened?

Traumatic events may involve actual or threatened physical or psychological harm, including violence, disasters, or severe neglect. They may occur as a single event or repeatedly over time.

Experience

How was it experienced?

An individual's perception and interpretation of an event influence whether it is experienced as traumatic. This experience may be shaped by cultural beliefs, social support, developmental stage, and feelings of fear, shame, or powerlessness.

Effect

What impact did it have?

Trauma can have lasting adverse effects on an individual's emotional, physical, cognitive, and social functioning. These effects may emerge immediately or develop over time.

Trauma is therefore not defined only by what happens, but also by how an individual experiences the event and its lasting impact (SAMHSA, 2014).

A moment to reflect

Looking at both definitions, is there anything you would add to or reconsider about your initial understanding?

Types and Sources of Trauma

Trauma can take different forms depending on the nature, duration, and context of traumatic experiences.

Types of Trauma

Acute Trauma

Results from a single distressing event, such as a serious accident or natural disaster.

Chronic Trauma

Develops through repeated or prolonged exposure to distressing experiences, such as ongoing violence or abuse.

Complex Trauma

Involves multiple or repeated traumatic experiences, often occurring within interpersonal relationships during childhood, potentially affecting development and attachment.

Common Sources of Trauma

Abuse & Neglect

Physical, emotional, or sexual abuse and neglect.

Domestic & Community Violence

Domestic and community violence.

War, Displacement & Disasters

War, forced displacement, and natural disasters.

Accidents, Illness & Loss

Serious accidents, life-threatening illness, or sudden loss.

Discrimination & Oppression

Discrimination, racism, and other forms of systemic oppression.

Impacts of Trauma on Individuals and Relationships

Trauma can influence multiple aspects of a person's life, extending beyond immediate emotional distress.

Explore the Impacts

Select an area below to explore how trauma may affect different aspects of life.

Emotional and Psychological Well-being

Anxiety, fear, shame, emotional numbness, or heightened alertness.

Resilience and Recovery

Importantly, trauma does not affect everyone in the same way. Supportive relationships, appropriate services, and personal strengths can promote resilience and recovery (SAMHSA, 2014).

Trauma, Loss and Grief

Trauma, loss and grief are closely connected because traumatic experiences may involve significant losses, while some experiences of loss may also be traumatic. However, these are distinct concepts and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Understanding Loss and Grief

Loss

The experience of losing something valued, such as a loved one, relationship, health, identity, sense of security or future opportunities. Loss is not limited to death.

Grief

A personal and multidimensional response to actual, perceived or anticipated loss. It may involve emotional, physical and social changes (Goldsworthy, 2005).

Grief does not follow a fixed sequence of stages or a universal timeline. Responses are shaped by personal meaning, relationships and sociocultural context (Goldsworthy, 2005; Neimeyer, 2016).

02 · Understanding Trauma-Informed Practice

What Is Trauma-Informed Practice?

The Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) is a US government agency working in mental health and substance use. In 2014, SAMHSA published guidance that brought together research, professional knowledge and the perspectives of trauma survivors to establish a shared framework for trauma-informed services. This framework provides the foundation for the following submodules (SAMHSA, 2014).

SAMHSA's Definition

SAMHSA describes a trauma-informed organisation or system as one that realises the widespread impact of trauma, recognises possible signs, responds by integrating trauma knowledge into policies and practices, and actively seeks to resist re-traumatisation.

Paraphrased from SAMHSA (2014, p. 9).

What Changes in a Trauma-Informed Service?

Imagine someone attending their first appointment at a community support service. Compare how the appointment might be organised.

Routine Intake
  • A standard form is provided before the appointment.
  • The worker follows a fixed sequence of assessment questions.
  • Adjustments depend mainly on individual staff discretion.
Trauma-Informed Intake
  • The appointment purpose, information use and available choices are clearly explained.
  • Preferences for pacing, breaks and participation are discussed.
  • Organisational policies and staff training support safe, flexible engagement.

Understanding the TIP Framework

SAMHSA's approach is built on two complementary elements: four key assumptions and six guiding principles (SAMHSA, 2014).

4 Rs — Key Assumptions

Realise, Recognise, Respond and Resist re-traumatisation

6 — Guiding Principles

Principles that guide safety, trust, collaboration, empowerment and more

The Four Rs of a Trauma-Informed Approach

SAMHSA (2014) identifies four key assumptions of a trauma-informed approach, known as the Four Rs. These are not four steps to follow in a fixed order. Instead, they describe how practitioners and organisations understand trauma, integrate this awareness into their work and seek to prevent re-traumatisation.

Understanding the Four Rs

Four interconnected assumptions that guide trauma-informed services.

Realise

Understand the widespread impact of trauma on individuals, families, communities and organisations, including possible pathways to recovery.

Recognise

Recognise possible signs of trauma among people accessing services, families, staff and others, while avoiding unsupported assumptions.

Respond

Integrate knowledge about trauma into everyday practice, organisational policies, procedures and staff support.

Resist Re-traumatisation

Actively identify and reduce practices or environments that may re-traumatise people receiving services or staff.

SAMHSA's Six Principles

SAMHSA (2014) identifies six interconnected principles that guide a trauma-informed approach across services and organisations. Rather than prescribing specific techniques, these principles provide a foundation for how people are supported, decisions are made and services are delivered. They complement the Four Rs introduced in the previous submodule.

Understanding the Six Principles

01
Safety

Create environments where people feel physically and psychologically safe, considering safety from their own perspectives.

In social workConsider privacy, the physical setting and what helps a person feel safe during an appointment.

02
Trustworthiness and Transparency

Build and maintain trust through honest, clear and consistent communication and organisational decision-making.

In social workExplain the purpose of an assessment and clarify how information may be shared.

03
Peer Support

Recognise the value of mutual support among people with relevant lived experience in fostering hope, connection and recovery.

In social workOffer access to appropriate peer-led support where available and welcomed.

04
Collaboration and Mutuality

Build partnerships, recognise everyone's contribution and address power differences through shared decision-making.

In social workDevelop a care plan with a person rather than making decisions for them.

05
Empowerment, Voice and Choice

Recognise strengths and support meaningful participation, choice and self-advocacy.

In social workInvite people to express their own needs, goals and preferences during assessment.

06
Cultural, Historical and Gender Issues

Challenge stereotypes and discrimination, recognise historical trauma and respond to cultural and gender-related needs.

In social workConsider how cultural identity, discrimination or historical experiences may shape engagement with services.

These six principles are interconnected and should guide both professional relationships and organisational practice.

Source: SAMHSA (2014, pp. 10–11).

Trauma-Informed Practice vs Trauma-Specific Interventions

When first encountering the term trauma-informed practice, it may be easy to assume that it refers to therapeutic interventions designed to treat trauma. Yet there is a fundamental distinction between trauma-informed practice and trauma-specific interventions.

Trauma-specific interventions are designed to directly address the psychological and emotional consequences of traumatic experiences. These may include therapeutic approaches that help individuals manage trauma-related symptoms, process distressing memories, and develop emotional regulation skills.

Trauma-informed practice, in contrast, is a broader approach to how services are designed and delivered. Rather than focusing primarily on treating trauma-related symptoms, it involves recognising the potential impact of trauma and creating service environments that promote safety, trust, participation, and recovery.

Harris and Fallot (2001) emphasise that trauma-informed services can be provided across different settings, including mental health services, housing support, and employment assistance, regardless of whether trauma treatment is their primary purpose.

Key Differences

Adapted from Harris & Fallot (2001).

Primary goal
Trauma-Informed Practice

To provide safe, responsive, and supportive services that recognise the impact of trauma.

Trauma-Specific Interventions

To directly address trauma-related symptoms and consequences.

Main focus
Trauma-Informed Practice

How services are organised, delivered, and experienced by clients.

Trauma-Specific Interventions

Therapeutic treatment of trauma-related difficulties.

Approach
Trauma-Informed Practice

Integrating trauma awareness into everyday interactions, organisational policies, and service delivery.

Trauma-Specific Interventions

Using targeted therapeutic techniques, such as grounding, desensitisation, and emotional regulation strategies.

Application
Trauma-Informed Practice

Relevant across a wide range of services, whether or not they provide trauma treatment.

Trauma-Specific Interventions

Provided when targeted treatment of trauma-related difficulties is appropriate.

Who is involved?
Trauma-Informed Practice

All levels of an organisation, including frontline workers, administrative staff, and management.

Trauma-Specific Interventions

Practitioners with appropriate training in trauma-specific therapeutic interventions.

An Analogy: “Disability-Informed”

An analogy may help make this distinction clearer.

Think about the ways public spaces accommodate people with disabilities. Museums provide wheelchair access, theatres offer sign-language interpreted performances, and restaurants reserve accessible parking spaces for people with mobility difficulties.

These organisations are not providing disability-specific services or rehabilitation. Rather, they are adapting their environments and the way they deliver services to accommodate different needs. In this sense, they are becoming “disability-informed” (Harris & Fallot, 2001, p. 5).

The same idea applies to trauma-informed practice.

A human service does not necessarily have to provide specialised trauma treatment to be trauma-informed. Instead, it can recognise how traumatic experiences may affect the people accessing its services and adapt its environment, policies, and everyday interactions accordingly.

Being trauma-informed is not simply about what services are provided, but how they are provided.

How Can TIP Support Social Work Practice?

Trauma-Informed Practice (TIP) can support social work in various ways. Two particularly relevant contributions are its alignment with professional ethics and its potential to inform anti-oppressive practice.

01 · PROFESSIONAL ETHICS

Putting Social Work Values into Practice

TIP aligns with the AASW's core ethical principles:

  • Respect for Persons
  • Social Justice
  • Professional Integrity

It supports ethical practice by encouraging social workers to promote dignity, meaningful participation and the responsible use of professional authority.

When professional responsibilities and client preferences create tensions, TIP provides guidance for approaching decisions transparently and collaboratively, without replacing professional ethical judgement.

AASW (2020); Levenson (2020)

02 · CRITICAL PERSPECTIVE

Recognising Power and Oppression

Social work takes place within systems shaped by professional power, institutional rules and unequal access to resources.

TIP encourages practitioners to recognise power imbalances and support shared decision-making (SAMHSA, 2014; Levenson, 2020).

A critical distinction: Trauma-informed approaches do not automatically address structural oppression. An anti-oppressive perspective encourages social workers to examine discrimination, inequality and institutional barriers, while critically reflecting on their own assumptions, values and position of power.

Leotti & Wahab (2025)

03 · Applying TIP in Social Work

Building Safe and Trusting Relationships

What can you do as a social worker?

Introduce yourself and clarify your role.

Explain who you are, why you are meeting with the client and what they can expect from the conversation.

Ask how the client would like to be addressed.

Check the name, title or pronouns the client prefers, rather than assuming. Use their chosen form of address consistently.

Use open-ended, non-judgemental questions.

Invite clients to share their concerns in their own words, rather than making assumptions about their experiences or needs.

Listen actively and acknowledge feelings.

Demonstrate genuine interest through attentive listening, appropriate responses and validation of the client's feelings.

Allow clients to engage at their own pace.

Respect silence, hesitation and discomfort. Avoid pressuring clients to share personal experiences before they feel ready, while explaining any information needed for immediate safety or service requirements.

Explain confidentiality and its limits clearly.

Describe who may access their information, how records are used and the circumstances in which information may need to be shared. Check that the client has an opportunity to ask questions.

Communicate consistently and follow through on commitments.

Agree on realistic follow-up arrangements, keep your promises and let the client know promptly when something changes or cannot be delivered.

Respect personal space and non-verbal cues.

Notice signs of discomfort without making assumptions about their meaning. Avoid unnecessary physical proximity or touch, and ask about preferences when appropriate.

Set clear and supportive professional boundaries.

Explain contact methods, availability and the scope of your role. Be warm and responsive without promising support beyond professional or service limits.

Acknowledge mistakes and address concerns openly.

If you miss a commitment or make an error, acknowledge it without defensiveness, explain the next steps and take appropriate action to repair trust.

Practice Scenario 1

You are a social work student meeting a client, Alex, for the first time at a community support service.

After introducing yourself, you notice that Alex avoids eye contact and responds to your questions with short answers.

When you ask what brings them to the service, Alex shrugs and says:

“I don't really know where to start.”

How would you respond?

Consider the client's pace

A structured assessment may be necessary, but immediately directing the conversation towards background questions could increase pressure before Alex feels comfortable. A trauma-informed approach allows space for rapport and invites the client to identify what feels manageable to discuss.

A trauma-informed response

This response acknowledges Alex's uncertainty without judgement and reduces the pressure to disclose everything at once. It also uses an open-ended invitation that gives Alex a meaningful role in directing the conversation. This can help support both emotional safety and early trust.

Reassurance is not always enough

Although the intention is supportive, this response assumes Alex is nervous and may minimise their uncertainty. Rather than telling clients how they should feel, trauma-informed engagement focuses on listening, acknowledging their experience and allowing them to express concerns in their own way.

Practice Scenario 2

You are meeting with Sam, a client who has recently started receiving support from your service.

During the conversation, Sam notices that you are taking notes and asks:

“Are those notes private? Who else gets to see what I tell you?”

How would you respond?

Avoid making absolute promises

This response may sound reassuring, but it promises absolute confidentiality. Social workers have legal and professional responsibilities that can require information sharing in certain circumstances. Making promises that cannot always be kept may damage trust.

A trauma-informed response

This response takes Sam's concern seriously and offers clear, honest information without making unrealistic promises. Explaining confidentiality and its limits helps clients understand what to expect and make more informed choices about what they share. The explanation should reflect the service's actual policies and legal obligations.

Transparency should not be postponed

Following organisational procedures is important, but postponing an answer to a direct question about privacy may increase uncertainty. Addressing Sam's concern before continuing demonstrates respect and helps establish a more predictable and trustworthy relationship.

Practice Scenario 3

You have been working with Riley, a client receiving community support.

Last week, you promised to send Riley information about a local support group, but you forgot.

At your next meeting, Riley says:

“You said you'd send it, but I never heard from you. Maybe I should just have your personal number so I can reach you directly.”

How would you respond?

A trauma-informed response

This response acknowledges the mistake without becoming defensive and takes responsibility for repairing trust. It also maintains a clear professional boundary while offering a reliable way to stay in contact. Transparency, accountability and consistent follow-through can help clients feel respected and secure in the helping relationship.

Boundaries need to be supportive

Maintaining professional contact arrangements is appropriate. However, focusing on how busy you have been can sound dismissive and does not acknowledge the missed commitment. A trauma-informed response combines clear boundaries with accountability, empathy and practical alternatives.

Avoid compensating by crossing boundaries

The intention is caring, but sharing personal contact details to compensate for a mistake may blur professional boundaries and create unrealistic expectations about availability. A more supportive response is to acknowledge the mistake, follow through on commitments and agree on appropriate contact arrangements.

Understanding and Responding to Client Behaviour

What can you do as a social worker?

Describe observable behaviour without labelling the client.

Describe what the client does or says rather than using labels such as "difficult", "unmotivated" or "non-compliant". Separate your observations from your interpretations.

Consider the client's circumstances before drawing conclusions about their behaviour.

Explore current stressors, family circumstances and recent experiences that may help explain the client's behaviour. Avoid making judgements based on a single interaction.

Explore how behaviour may function as a coping or protective strategy.

Consider whether avoidance, withdrawal, anger or other responses might have helped the client manage difficult experiences. Explore these possibilities without assuming that every behaviour is trauma-related.

Ask about barriers before interpreting missed appointments as disengagement.

Check whether transport, caring responsibilities, scheduling difficulties or previous negative service experiences have affected attendance before making assumptions about the client's motivation.

Consider how systemic barriers, discrimination or unmet support needs may affect engagement.

Explore whether difficulties with service access, experiences of discrimination, or unmet cultural, linguistic or practical needs may be influencing the client's behaviour or participation. Avoid interpreting disengagement solely as an individual problem.

Respond to guardedness or resistance with curiosity rather than confrontation.

Ask what may be making participation difficult instead of challenging the client's attitude. Avoid responses that shame, threaten or escalate a power struggle.

Explore ambivalence without pressuring the client to change.

Acknowledge that clients may have mixed feelings about changing familiar coping strategies. Invite them to discuss both the benefits and concerns associated with change.

Recognise signs of emotional distress and adjust your approach.

Notice changes in tone, agitation, withdrawal or difficulty concentrating. Consider slowing the conversation, reducing demands or offering a pause when appropriate.

Use calm communication and offer practical options for de-escalation.

Speak calmly, avoid threatening language or unnecessary physical contact, and offer manageable options such as taking a break or moving to a quieter space when safe and appropriate.

Validate feelings while maintaining appropriate safety limits.

Acknowledge the client's feelings without approving harmful behaviour. Explain any necessary safety boundaries respectfully and follow relevant safeguarding procedures.

Practice Scenario 1

You are a social worker at a family support service. A client, Talia, has missed two appointments.

A colleague describes Talia as "unmotivated". When you contact her, she explains:

“The letters are hard for me to understand, and my work shifts finish after your office closes.”

How would you respond?

Look beyond attendance

Although attendance expectations may need to be explained, this response does not explore the barriers Talia has identified. Focusing immediately on compliance may reinforce the assumption that attendance difficulties reflect a lack of motivation.

Avoid assuming trauma is the explanation

Previous negative experiences could affect engagement, but Talia has already identified practical and communication barriers. Suggesting a trauma-related explanation without evidence may overlook her actual circumstances and needs.

Explore the context before drawing conclusions

This response takes Talia's explanation seriously and explores the circumstances affecting her participation. It avoids labelling her as unmotivated and recognises that service accessibility, communication barriers and working conditions can influence engagement.

Practice Scenario 2

You are working with Sam, a client attending a court-mandated alcohol support programme.

During a session, Sam says:

“I know drinking causes problems sometimes, but it's the only thing that helps me switch off. I don't want someone telling me to stop.”

How would you respond?

Confrontation may increase defensiveness

This response focuses on correcting the client rather than understanding their perspective. It may evoke shame or defensiveness and overlooks the possibility that alcohol has become a familiar way of coping with stress. Accountability can be discussed without confrontation.

Explore ambivalence with curiosity

This response acknowledges the possible coping function of drinking without endorsing harmful use. It invites Sam to explore mixed feelings and concerns about change, rather than imposing a conclusion. Any mandated requirements and immediate safety concerns still need to be addressed.

Acceptance does not mean avoiding difficult topics

Respecting the client's concerns is important, but completely avoiding the issue may overlook the purpose of the service and any mandatory requirements. A trauma-informed approach allows exploration of difficult topics without shaming or pressuring the client.

Practice Scenario 3

You are a school social worker meeting with Kai, a 15-year-old student referred after a conflict with a teacher.

During the conversation, Kai becomes visibly agitated, raises their voice and stands up.

“Nobody at this school ever listens to me! I'm done talking!”

How would you respond?

Support de-escalation while attending to safety

This response acknowledges the student's distress, reduces immediate conversational demands and offers manageable options. It avoids confrontation and unnecessary pressure. The social worker should also assess immediate safety and follow relevant school safeguarding procedures.

Avoid directing the client's emotional response

Although breathing may help some people regulate distress, instructing Kai to calm down can feel dismissive or controlling. Strategies for regulation are better offered as options rather than imposed. First attend to safety and respond calmly to the student's distress.

Threats can escalate the situation

Emphasising consequences during a moment of distress may intensify fear, anger or a power struggle. Safety and school responsibilities still matter, but these should be addressed calmly, without unnecessary threats or humiliation.

Conducting Trauma-Informed Assessments

What can you do as a social worker?

Explain the purpose and process of the assessment.

Tell clients why the assessment is needed, what information you will ask for and how it will be used. Explain what they can expect before beginning.

Seek informed consent before discussing sensitive experiences, where appropriate.

Explain the purpose of sensitive questions and ask whether the client is comfortable proceeding. Be clear about any questions or information-sharing requirements that cannot be optional.

Begin with the client's presenting concerns rather than assuming trauma is the primary issue.

Ask what has brought the client to the service and what concerns them currently. Avoid assuming that their difficulties must be explained by past trauma.

Ask only for information relevant to the assessment and avoid unnecessary probing.

Focus on information needed to understand the client's circumstances, identify risks and determine appropriate support. Avoid requesting detailed trauma narratives when they are not necessary.

Offer breaks or pauses when discussing distressing experiences.

Notice signs of discomfort and check whether the client would like a pause or a change of pace. Where possible, allow sensitive information to be explored across more than one session.

Respond to trauma disclosures with validation rather than immediate requests for details.

Acknowledge what the client has shared without judgement. Avoid pressing for a detailed account unless further information is necessary for immediate safety or professional responsibilities.

Check for immediate safety concerns without requiring a detailed trauma narrative.

Ask focused questions about current safety when concerns arise. Follow relevant safeguarding and reporting procedures without unnecessarily revisiting distressing experiences.

Explore strengths, coping strategies and supportive relationships alongside difficulties.

Ask what has helped the client manage challenges, what they feel they do well and who they can turn to for support. Include these strengths alongside identified concerns.

Use language and identity-related terminology preferred by the client.

Ask about and respect the client's preferred terms when discussing their identity, relationships and experiences. Use these terms consistently in assessment conversations and documentation, avoiding assumptions or stigmatising labels.

Summarise what you have heard and check your understanding with the client.

Reflect back the main points of the assessment and ask whether your understanding is accurate. Invite clients to clarify or correct information before finalising conclusions.

Document assessment information using respectful, non-stigmatising language.

Record relevant observations and client-reported information accurately. Distinguish facts from interpretations, avoid judgemental labels and document strengths alongside concerns.

Practice Scenario 1

You are a social work student completing an intake assessment at a community support service.

Jordan has come in for emergency rent assistance. The intake form includes questions about family and personal history.

Jordan looks at the form and asks:

“Why do you need to know all that? I just need help with my rent.”

How would you respond?

Explain the purpose, not just the procedure

Although the questions may be part of a standard assessment, this response does not explain why the information is needed. Referring only to procedure may leave Jordan feeling that their concerns are being overlooked. A trauma-informed approach makes the assessment process understandable and avoids unnecessary questioning.

Explain why the information is needed

This response acknowledges Jordan's concern and connects the questions to the support they are seeking. It helps the client understand the purpose of the assessment without assuming that all personal history must be explored immediately. Any required information should still be collected and explained clearly.

Avoid making promises you may not be able to keep

Allowing flexibility can support client comfort, but some information may be necessary to assess eligibility or safety. Skipping an entire section without checking these requirements could create problems later. Explain which questions are essential and which can be postponed.

Practice Scenario 2

You are a social worker conducting an assessment with Maya at a family support service.

While talking about their home situation, Maya tells you:

“My ex used to lock me in the house. I don't really want to go into it.”

How would you respond?

Avoid unnecessary requests for trauma details

Acknowledging the disclosure is helpful, but immediately asking for a detailed account may place pressure on Maya after they have said they do not want to discuss it. A more appropriate first step is to validate the experience and identify whether any immediate safety information is needed.

Acknowledge the disclosure and check current safety

This response acknowledges Maya's experience without pressing for unnecessary details. It also checks current safety, which may be important for deciding what support or protective action is needed. Further assessment should follow relevant safeguarding and reporting responsibilities.

Respecting disclosure boundaries still requires professional judgement

Moving away from a sensitive topic may respect the client's stated preference, but completely overlooking the disclosure could mean missing an immediate safety concern. A brief, focused safety check may be necessary without requiring the client to describe the past experience in detail.

Practice Scenario 3

You are finishing an assessment with Morgan, a parent receiving family support.

Morgan reads your draft summary and says:

“It says I've been struggling to cope. But I've kept the kids in school, and my sister helps with the afternoons. That makes it sound like I'm doing nothing right.”

How would you respond?

Professional wording can still affect clients

This response attempts to reassure Morgan but dismisses their concern about how they are represented. Assessment records should be accurate, respectful and attentive to strengths as well as difficulties. Standard wording is not a reason to overlook the client's perspective.

Include strengths and check the accuracy of your assessment

This response acknowledges Morgan's concern and recognises the coping efforts and family support they have described. It also invites the client to check the written summary. A balanced assessment documents strengths alongside relevant needs and risks rather than focusing only on difficulties.

A balanced assessment should not omit relevant concerns

Removing all references to difficulties may make the summary less accurate. Trauma-informed documentation is not about recording only positive information; it involves representing both challenges and strengths fairly. A better response would review the wording with Morgan and clarify what the assessment needs to capture.

Supporting Client Autonomy and Shared Decision-Making

What can you do as a social worker?

Ask clients which concerns they would like to address first.

When clients face multiple difficulties, ask what feels most urgent or important to them. Consider their priorities alongside any immediate safety concerns that require attention.

Explore the client's preferences, values and goals before recommending actions.

Ask what matters to the client, what they have already tried and what they hope to achieve. Avoid assuming that your preferred solution is necessarily the best fit for their circumstances.

Explain available options, including their potential benefits, risks and limitations.

Provide clear information about relevant services, eligibility requirements, waiting times and possible consequences. Help clients compare options without pressuring them towards one particular choice.

Check the client's understanding before asking them to make a decision.

Use accessible language and invite questions. Check whether the client understands the options and what each involves, rather than assuming that agreement or silence indicates informed understanding.

Be transparent about non-negotiable requirements while identifying choices that remain.

Explain any legal, safeguarding or service requirements that cannot be changed. Where possible, involve clients in deciding how these requirements are met and what support they would find helpful.

Identify organisational constraints and advocate for more accessible or equitable options where appropriate.

Recognise when service policies, eligibility requirements or resource limitations restrict the client's choices. Discuss available alternatives and, with the client's involvement, advocate for adjustments or more accessible services where appropriate.

Invite clients to define their own goals rather than imposing professional priorities.

Ask what meaningful progress would look like from the client's perspective. Develop goals that reflect their priorities and circumstances while addressing relevant professional responsibilities.

Break agreed goals into manageable steps together.

Work with clients to identify realistic actions they can take towards their goals. Consider available resources, current capacity and practical barriers when deciding what can be achieved.

Clarify responsibilities and next steps through collaborative planning.

Agree on who will do what, when actions will take place and how progress will be followed up. Make sure the client understands and has a say in the agreed arrangements.

Support clients to advocate for themselves rather than taking over decisions or tasks.

Offer practical support such as preparing for conversations, explaining procedures or attending meetings together. Encourage client participation without assuming they must manage everything independently.

Review decisions and adjust plans based on client feedback.

Regularly ask whether agreed actions are working and whether the client's priorities have changed. Make adjustments collaboratively rather than continuing with a plan that no longer meets their needs.

Practice Scenario 1

You are a social worker at a family support service. Nadia is dealing with housing insecurity, financial stress and concerns about her son's school attendance.

During your meeting, Nadia says:

“I know there's a lot going on. But honestly, I just need to work out where we're going to stay next week.”

How would you respond?

More referrals are not always the best starting point

Although several referrals may eventually be helpful, this response moves ahead with a professional plan without first exploring Nadia's immediate priorities. A trauma-informed approach involves the client in deciding where to focus, rather than trying to address everything at once.

Avoid replacing client priorities with professional assumptions

Nadia's emotional wellbeing matters, but she has clearly identified housing as her immediate concern. Prioritising stress management without discussing her preference may leave her feeling unheard. Professional judgement should be considered alongside client-defined needs.

Start with the client's priority while checking immediate safety

This response recognises what matters most to Nadia and checks an immediate safety consideration. It offers a starting point without taking over the entire plan. Client priorities can guide the work while the social worker continues to attend to relevant safety responsibilities.

Practice Scenario 2

You are supporting Casey, a parent involved with a family support service.

Attending a parenting course is a requirement in Casey's current support plan. However, the sessions run during Casey's working hours.

Casey tells you:

“I'm not losing shifts to go to this course. I need that money. What am I supposed to do?”

How would you respond?

Look for meaningful choices within the requirements

This response acknowledges the practical conflict without suggesting Casey can simply ignore the plan. It distinguishes the requirement from how it might be fulfilled and explores opportunities for negotiation and advocacy. An alternative cannot be guaranteed, but the worker can help Casey participate in finding a workable solution.

Consider whose circumstances are expected to change

This response explains the requirement and suggests a possible solution, but it places responsibility on Casey to adjust their employment. Before recommending this, the social worker should explore other programme options and whether the service itself can accommodate the client's circumstances.

Advocacy requires clarity about professional authority

Questioning an inaccessible requirement may be appropriate, but the social worker cannot assume the course can simply be removed. Effective advocacy involves checking the actual requirements, discussing Casey's preferences and negotiating feasible alternatives without making promises beyond the worker's authority.

Practice Scenario 3

You are working with Ellis at a community support service.

You have agreed to help Ellis apply for financial hardship assistance with an overdue electricity bill.

When you look through the application together, Ellis says:

“I want to get this sorted, but there's so much paperwork. I don't think I can get through all of it this week.”

How would you respond?

Helping does not always mean taking over

Completing paperwork for a client may sometimes be appropriate, particularly when accessibility or urgency requires it. However, offering to take over before exploring what Ellis wants may reduce their involvement. The social worker should ask what assistance would be most useful rather than assuming full responsibility.

Break goals into achievable, shared steps

This response turns an agreed goal into smaller, realistic actions while keeping Ellis involved. It offers practical assistance without assuming they must manage the task alone. The next step would be to agree on responsibilities, check any deadlines and review progress together.

A plan should reflect the client's capacity and input

A clear deadline can be useful, but this response sets the pace and tasks without exploring Ellis's concerns. Collaborative planning involves checking what is achievable, identifying barriers and agreeing on responsibilities together, rather than assigning tasks unilaterally.
